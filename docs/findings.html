Do birds and plants draw the same map?

Bioregions derived from citizen-science occurrence records on the US East Coast, clustered from species composition alone — no climate, elevation, or political geography as input. Two questions: does the result agree with expert-drawn ecoregions, and do independent groups of organisms agree with each other?

Finding 1 — Congruence

Two unrelated biotas converge, and converge harder at finer grain

Birds and plants were clustered separately, sharing nothing but the grid. At eight regions they agree with each other (0.46) more than either agrees with the expert framework (≈0.29) — which is what you would expect if both are tracking real biogeography and the expert map is a different, coarser abstraction of it.

Where they disagree is interpretable

At the coarsest cut the two clades place their primary boundary differently. Birds isolate peninsular Florida and its subtropical avifauna; plants split at the coastal plain boundary near 34°N. Same region, different dominant signal.

Finding 2 — The record/taxon inversion

91% of the records are birds. 11× more of the species are plants.

eBird dominates GBIF's East Coast holdings so heavily that it is tempting to call the combined map a bird map. That is wrong, and the reason matters: presence/absence composition is built over taxa, not records. Birds bring overwhelming volume from about 4,300 species; plants bring 47,000 species from a fraction of the records.

Consequence. The combined map's two-region split (34.4–39.6°N) resembles the plant map, not the bird map, despite birds supplying 97% of the records that went into it. Record share is not taxon share.

Finding 3 — Validation and grain

The chosen number of regions was the worst one

The pipeline selects the number of regions by a weighted cluster-quality score, which lands on two. Against an independently drawn framework, two is the weakest cut it could have picked — four agrees more than twice as well. Homogeneity rises with grain while completeness falls, so no single cut serves every purpose, which is the argument for publishing the nesting instead of one answer.

Changing the dissimilarity index mattered more than anything else tried

Observed richness in this data is largely sampling effort — a hexagon's record count and its species count correlate at 0.975. Sørensen dissimilarity is sensitive to richness differences, so it reported hexagons as different because one was visited more. Simpson's βsim removes that component, and costs no data.

What this does not show

Birds and plants were run at their natural volumes, which differ by 36×. A matched-volume control — sampling birds down to the plant record count — has not been run, so part of the disagreement at coarse grain may be resolution rather than biology. The bird map is also built from a much narrower feature space (1,070 taxa after filtering, against 9,061 for plants), which is a property of avian diversity rather than of sampling and would not be fixed by matching volumes.

Agreement with EPA Level I is approximately zero (−0.038). That is expected rather than a failure: Level I places nearly this entire extent in a single region, so there is no partition to agree with.
